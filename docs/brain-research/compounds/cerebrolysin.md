> RESEARCH-GRADE / Rx FLAG: Cerebrolysin is a porcine brain-derived peptide preparation given by **intravenous or intramuscular** administration. It is a prescription drug in some countries and is **NOT an oral nootropic supplement**. It cannot be self-administered safely. Nothing here is medical advice.

## Summary

Cerebrolysin is a peptidergic preparation derived from purified porcine brain proteins, marketed in parts of Europe, Asia and Latin America (not FDA-approved in the US) as a "neurotrophic" agent. It has been studied primarily in **disease populations** — vascular dementia, Alzheimer's disease, acute ischemic stroke, and traumatic brain injury. The clinical evidence is **mixed and modest at best**, and is materially limited by study heterogeneity, high risk of bias, and publication-bias concerns (much of the literature originates from a small number of groups and the manufacturer). There is **no credible evidence for cognitive enhancement in healthy people**, and the parenteral route, cost, and adverse-event profile make it inappropriate for that use. Graded **2 (limited/mixed evidence, in impaired populations only)**.

## Mechanism

Cerebrolysin is a heterogeneous mixture of low-molecular-weight neuropeptides and free amino acids. Its proposed mechanism is **neurotrophic mimicry** — the peptide fraction is hypothesized to act like endogenous neurotrophic factors (BDNF/GDNF/NGF-like signaling), supporting neuronal survival, synaptic plasticity, neurogenesis, and providing anti-apoptotic/neuroprotective effects in ischemia and injury models. These mechanisms are largely **preclinical/mechanistic** and have not been cleanly tied to clinical benefit in humans. The primary channel is therefore **ntrophic** (neurotrophic-mimetic); a secondary, weaker **inflam** (neuroprotective/anti-inflammatory) signal is preclinical only.

## Evidence

**Vascular dementia (Cochrane).** The Cochrane review (Cui et al., 2019; PMID: 31710397; DOI: 10.1002/14651858.CD008900.pub3) found that IV courses of Cerebrolysin were associated with improvements in cognition and general function with no clear signal of adverse effects — but the authors explicitly cautioned that analyses were **limited by heterogeneity** and **included studies had high risk of bias**, concluding that "if there are benefits... the effects may be too small to be clinically meaningful," and that adequately powered, robust trials are needed. No new eligible trials had appeared since the prior version.

**Alzheimer's disease (meta-analyses, mixed).** A meta-analysis of RCTs in mild-to-moderate AD (Gauthier et al., 2015; PMID: 25832905; DOI: 10.1159/000377672) reported an overall beneficial effect and favorable benefit-risk ratio on cognition and global clinical change. An earlier meta-analysis (Wei et al., 2007; PMID: 17318304; DOI: 10.1007/s00702-007-0630-y) similarly reported benefit. These positive findings must be weighed against **small trial counts, sponsor involvement, and publication-bias concerns**, so the AD signal is best read as **suggestive, not definitive**.

**Acute ischemic stroke (Cochrane — largely negative/cautionary).** The Cochrane review (Ziganshina et al., 2020; PMID: 32662068; DOI: 10.1002/14651858.CD007026.pub6; updated 2023, PMID: 37818733) found **moderate-quality evidence of little or no benefit** on all-cause death and a **possible increase in non-fatal serious adverse events**. An independent meta-analysis (Wang et al., 2017; PMID: 28458521; DOI: 10.2147/DDDT.S124273) found **no significant improvement in functional recovery** at day 90 and concluded routine use is not supported. European Stroke Organisation / European Academy of Neurology guidance advises **against** Cerebrolysin for post-stroke cognitive impairment.

**TBI.** Some small RCTs (e.g., combined Cerebrolysin + rTMS three-arm trial, Front Neurosci 2023; DOI: 10.3389/fnins.2023.1186751) report tolerability and exploratory signals, but evidence is preliminary and underpowered for efficacy.

**Bias caveat (applies across indications).** A large share of the positive literature comes from a limited number of investigators and from manufacturer-associated trials; heterogeneity in dose, course length, populations, and outcome scales is high; and high risk of bias is repeatedly flagged by Cochrane. Effect sizes, where positive, tend to be small.

**Healthy enhancement.** No controlled evidence supports cognitive enhancement in healthy individuals. This is an off-label/unstudied use and is not recommended.

## Safety & interactions

- **Route:** IV infusion or IM injection only — requires clinical administration; rapid IV infusion can cause flushing, dizziness, and sweating.
- **Hypersensitivity:** Contraindicated with known hypersensitivity; rare anaphylactoid/allergic reactions reported. Porcine-derived biologic with theoretical immunogenicity considerations.
- **MAO inhibitors:** Concomitant use **not recommended** (potential additive/summation effects); caution also advised with antidepressants.
- **Compatibility:** Do not co-infuse with balanced amino-acid solutions in the same line.
- **Cautions:** Status epilepticus / active seizure disorder, severe renal impairment, pregnancy/lactation (insufficient data).
- **Stroke signal:** Meta-analytic data raise a possible increase in non-fatal serious adverse events.

## Open questions

- Do any positive dementia/AD findings survive correction for publication bias and high risk of bias in adequately powered, independent (non-manufacturer) RCTs?
- Is there a dose/course regimen with a genuinely favorable benefit-risk ratio, and in which population?
- What is the actual pharmacology — which peptide fractions (if any) cross the blood-brain barrier and produce neurotrophic-mimetic effects in humans?
- Can the stroke serious-adverse-event signal be characterized and explained?
- No data address healthy cognitive enhancement, and the parenteral route makes such use impractical and unjustified.
