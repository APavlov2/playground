## Summary

Choline bitartrate is a salt of choline, an essential nutrient and the dietary precursor of both acetylcholine (ACh) and phosphatidylcholine. It is widely sold as a cheap "nootropic," but the nootropic claim is graded LOW. As a brain-choline delivery vehicle it is poor relative to alpha-GPC (choline alphoscerate) and citicoline (CDP-choline), which raise CNS/plasma choline more efficiently. Acute cognitive-enhancement evidence in healthy adults is weak-to-negative.

Importantly, distinguish two separate uses: (1) **dietary adequacy / deficiency correction** — choline is genuinely essential, with established roles in liver health and a plausible (but unproven in humans) role in fetal brain development; and (2) **acute nootropic use in already-replete healthy adults** — where benefit is not demonstrated. This entry grades the nootropic claim, not the dietary-essential status.

## Mechanism (poor CNS delivery vs alpha-GPC/citicoline)

Choline enters the brain across the blood-brain barrier via a saturable, relatively low-capacity transporter, and free choline from the bitartrate salt does not efficiently raise brain choline at typical doses. By contrast, alpha-GPC and citicoline are lipophilic/intermediate choline-carrying compounds that deliver choline (and, for citicoline, cytidine) more effectively and are the forms with cholinergic clinical data in impaired populations. Thus, while choline bitartrate nominally feeds the ACh and phosphatidylcholine pathways, the rate-limiting issue for a nootropic effect — getting choline into CNS cholinergic neurons — is where the bitartrate salt underperforms. The phosphatidylcholine (membrane) pathway is a matter of structural/dietary substrate supply rather than acute cognitive signaling.

## Evidence (weak nootropic; dietary adequacy/deficiency)

**Acute nootropic, healthy adults — negative.** A double-blind, placebo-controlled study (multiple experiments) gave healthy young adults ~2.0-2.5 g choline bitartrate ~1 h before testing and found no improvement in declarative, visuospatial, or working memory; Bayesian analysis supported the null (Lippelt et al., PLoS One 2016; PMID 27341028). This is the most directly relevant trial and is essentially null.

**Possible visuomotor signal — single, small, unreplicated.** One small placebo-controlled crossover (n=28, 2 g choline bitartrate) reported improved visuomotor tracking and pupil constriction (Naber et al., Sci Rep 2015; PMID 26271904). This is a narrow, non-memory outcome in a small sample and has not been robustly replicated; it does not establish a general cognitive-enhancement effect.

**Dietary adequacy / deficiency (distinct from nootropic use).** Choline was recognized as an essential nutrient in 1998; the Adequate Intake is 425 mg/day (women) and 550 mg/day (men), and deficiency causes hepatic fat accumulation / liver dysfunction (e.g., in parenteral-nutrition settings) (Wallace et al., Nutr Today 2018; PMID 30853718). Adequacy matters; this is not the same as an acute cognitive boost in replete people.

**Pregnancy / fetal brain (adequacy context, mixed human evidence).** Animal data suggest maternal choline benefits offspring cognition, but human RCTs are mixed and mostly null/inconclusive (e.g., phosphatidylcholine supplementation in moderate-choline-diet pregnant women did not enhance infant cognition, PMID 23134891; systematic review PMID 40077755). Evidence is insufficient to confirm a neurodevelopmental benefit beyond meeting adequacy.

## Safety & interactions (TMAO, fishy odor, GI, hypotension high-dose)

- **TMAO (cardiovascular signal).** Gut microbiota convert choline to trimethylamine, which the liver oxidizes to TMAO, a marker associated with cardiovascular/thrombotic risk. Choline bitartrate specifically raises TMAO more than phosphatidylcholine: in healthy men it produced ~3x greater plasma TMAO AUC and ~2.5x greater urinary TMAO than phosphatidylcholine/control (Cho et al., Nutrients 2020; PMID 32722424). Oral choline supplementation also raised fasting TMAO and enhanced platelet aggregation in humans (Wang et al., Circulation 2017; PMID 28438808). This is a relevant downside of the bitartrate form at supplemental doses.
- **Fishy body odor** from trimethylamine at high doses.
- **GI effects:** nausea, loose stools, abdominal discomfort; also cholinergic-type sweating and salivation.
- **Hypotension** has been reported at high doses (multi-gram). The Tolerable Upper Intake Level for total choline is 3.5 g/day in adults.
- contraindications/interactions: none well-established (unsourced) for specific drug interactions; the main flagged concern is the TMAO/platelet-reactivity signal at high intake.

## Open questions

- Does any subgroup (e.g., genuinely low-choline diets, certain MTHFD1/PEMT genotypes) get a cognitive benefit from choline bitartrate specifically, vs needing alpha-GPC/citicoline?
- Is the single visuomotor/pupil finding (PMID 26271904) replicable, and is it a true CNS-cholinergic effect or peripheral?
- What is the clinical significance of bitartrate-driven TMAO elevation for long-term supplement users?
- Head-to-head CNS-choline delivery and cognitive endpoints: choline bitartrate vs alpha-GPC vs citicoline in healthy adults (largely lacking).
