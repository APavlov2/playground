## Summary
Krill oil delivers marine omega-3s (EPA/DHA) bound largely in **phospholipid** form, plus a small amount of the carotenoid antioxidant **astaxanthin**. Its marketing rests on two claims: (1) better bioavailability than triglyceride fish oil, and (2) direct brain benefit. The honest reading is modest on both. Krill oil **shares the omega-3 (EPA/DHA) evidence base** documented in the `omega-3-epa-dha` entry — where the cognition-enhancement and dementia-prevention story is largely **null** in replete people, with genuine benefit confined mainly to **EPA-predominant supplementation in depression** and to deficiency correction. Krill-specific human **brain/cognition RCTs are very few**: one notable trial (Konagai 2013) in healthy elderly men suggested increased task-related cerebral activation and faster P300, but it is small (n=45), single, and not replicated. The bioavailability advantage is real-ish but **small and inconsistent** across trials. Graded **2** overall: a thin krill-specific dataset riding on a modest omega-3 base, not a standalone enhancer.

## Mechanism
**ntrophic (DHA structural, up; grade 2).** The active brain-relevant cargo is DHA (and EPA). DHA is the dominant n-3 PUFA of synaptic membranes, supporting membrane fluidity, receptor/signal-transduction efficiency and synaptogenesis (full mechanism in the omega-3 entry). Krill's distinguishing feature is the **phospholipid carrier** (phosphatidylcholine-rich), proposed to improve incorporation into plasma phospholipids and membranes. The structural biology is solid but is a **read-across from omega-3**, not krill-specific; and adding omega-3 to an already-replete adult brain is not shown to yield cognitive gain — hence grade 2 despite strong mechanism.

**inflam (EPA + astaxanthin, down; grade 2).** EPA/DHA feed specialised pro-resolving mediators and displace pro-inflammatory eicosanoids; krill adds **astaxanthin**, a lipophilic carotenoid antioxidant with anti-inflammatory activity that could in principle complement the omega-3 effect. However, the astaxanthin quantity in typical krill oil is **small**, the antioxidant/anti-inflammatory pathway to a measurable human brain endpoint is unproven, and this again largely read-across from the omega-3 base. Grade 2.

No credible krill-specific basis for assigning ach, da, ser (as a krill claim), glu, gaba, ne, cbf (Konagai's NIRS oxyhaemoglobin is a downstream activation readout, not a demonstrated cerebral-blood-flow mechanism), mito, glymph or hpa channels.

## Evidence
Distinguish krill-specific data (thin) from the inherited omega-3 base (modest, mostly null for cognition).

- **Brain-specific RCT (Konagai 2013, PMID:24098072):** the one notable krill brain trial. Randomized, n=45 healthy elderly men (61-72 y), 12 weeks, 2 g/day krill oil (~193 mg EPA + 92 mg DHA) vs sardine oil vs MCT placebo. During working-memory and calculation tasks, task-related **cerebral oxyhaemoglobin (NIRS)** rose more in krill (and sardine) vs placebo, and **P300 latency** was lower in krill vs placebo. Interpreted by the authors as omega-3 "activating" cognitive function, with phospholipid (krill) appearing at least as effective as triglyceride (sardine). Caveats: small, single, surrogate electrophysiological/haemodynamic endpoints rather than clinically meaningful cognitive outcomes, healthy population, no replication. Suggestive, not decisive — consistent with grade 2.

- **Bioavailability vs fish oil (mixed/modest):** Schuchardt 2011 (PMID:21854650), single-dose crossover n=12, found the **highest mean** plasma-phospholipid EPA+DHA incorporation with krill oil but **no statistically significant** difference vs fish oil rTAG or ethyl ester (high variance). Ramprasath 2013 (PMID:24304605), crossover n=24 at equal 600 mg n-3, found krill raised the **Omega-3 Index** more than fish oil. Net: a plausible **small** phospholipid advantage, more visible at lower doses and as membrane incorporation, but **inconsistent and modest** — not the large edge sometimes marketed.

- **Inherited omega-3 base (cross-reference `omega-3-epa-dha`; do NOT double-count):** the strongest brain evidence for EPA/DHA is EPA-predominant supplementation in **depression** (Liao 2019, PMID:31383846), while **cognition/prevention** trials are largely null in cognitively-normal elderly (Cochrane/Sydenham 2012, PMID:22696350) and in Alzheimer disease (Quinn 2010, PMID:21045096). Krill oil delivers the same EPA/DHA and therefore inherits this profile; it does not have its own depression or dementia-prevention RCT base. Krill's per-gram EPA/DHA is **lower** than concentrated fish oil, so matched omega-3 dosing requires more krill oil.

Net: `evidence_overall: 2`. One small positive brain RCT plus a modest, inconsistent bioavailability edge, layered on an omega-3 base that is itself only modestly supported for brain optimisation. Not graded higher because the krill-specific cognitive dataset is thin and unreplicated and most of the apparent support is borrowed from omega-3.

## Safety & interactions (research metadata)
- **Shellfish allergy (contraindication):** krill are **crustaceans**, so krill oil is contraindicated in people with crustacean/shellfish allergy. (Some sources note the major shellfish allergen may be low in the purified oil, but standard guidance is avoidance.)
- **Anticoagulants / antiplatelets (interaction):** as a marine omega-3 source, theoretical additive bleeding risk with warfarin, DOACs, aspirin or clopidogrel. Pooled omega-3 RCT data (see omega-3 entry, Javaid 2024, PMID:38742535) show no clear overall bleeding increase, so this is precautionary rather than strongly evidence-based — but prudent to flag.
- **GI / palatability:** mild GI upset and fishy reflux, as with fish oil.
- **Oxidation:** PUFAs oxidise; poorly stored product can carry oxidised lipids (a quality, not dose, issue). Astaxanthin may modestly retard oxidation.
- **Astaxanthin:** present in small amounts in krill oil; isolated high-dose astaxanthin effects (e.g. skin pigmentation) are not relevant at krill-oil exposures.

## Open questions
- Does krill oil's phospholipid form translate into any **brain-specific** advantage over triglyceride fish oil at matched EPA/DHA dose, or is the only difference modest plasma/RBC incorporation? No head-to-head cognitive RCT establishes a clinical edge.
- Can the Konagai 2013 NIRS/P300 activation signal be replicated with **clinically meaningful** cognitive endpoints and in mixed-sex, larger samples?
- Does the astaxanthin component add anything to the omega-3 anti-inflammatory effect at the small doses present, or is it negligible?
- As with omega-3 generally: is any benefit confined to **deficiency correction / low Omega-3 Index** rather than enhancement in replete people?
