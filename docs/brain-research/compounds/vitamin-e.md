## Summary

Vitamin E (alpha-tocopherol) is a fat-soluble, lipid-phase antioxidant. Its one credible cognitive signal is narrow: high-dose 2000 IU/day modestly slowed loss of activities of daily living in people who already have **mild-to-moderate Alzheimer's disease** (TEAM-AD, PMID 24381967). It does **not** prevent progression of MCI to AD (PMID 15829527) and does **not** prevent dementia in healthy older people (PREADViSE, PMID 28319243). The benefit is a treatment effect at one disease stage, not prevention, and it is offset by a real safety cost: high-dose vitamin E is associated with increased all-cause mortality (PMID 15537682) and increased hemorrhagic-stroke risk (PMID 21051774). Graded down accordingly.

## Mechanism

Alpha-tocopherol partitions into cell membranes and lipoproteins, where it scavenges lipid peroxyl radicals and terminates chain lipid peroxidation, then is regenerated by vitamin C and other reductants. The hypothesis in AD is that reducing oxidative damage to neuronal membranes slows neurodegeneration. This is plausibly why the only signal lands on a global/functional endpoint in established AD rather than on prevention. Mapped to the **inflam** channel as a lipid-antioxidant, graded modestly because the mechanism-to-outcome link is supported by a single positive functional trial and is direction-uncertain (modulate), not a clean cognitive-enhancement signal.

## Evidence

- **AD treatment (positive, narrow): TEAM-AD, Dysken 2014, JAMA, PMID 24381967.** Double-blind RCT, 613 patients with mild-to-moderate AD on a cholinesterase inhibitor, 14 VA centers. 2000 IU/day alpha-tocopherol slowed functional decline (ADCS-ADL) vs placebo. Memantine and the vitamin E + memantine combination did not show this benefit. This is the entry's strongest data point and the basis for grading the inflam channel.
- **MCI -> AD prevention (null): Petersen 2005, NEJM, PMID 15829527.** 769 subjects with MCI randomized to 2000 IU/day vitamin E, donepezil, or placebo for 3 years. Vitamin E showed **no** benefit on progression to AD at any time point.
- **Primary prevention in healthy people (null): PREADViSE, Kryscio 2017, JAMA Neurol, PMID 28319243.** 7,540 asymptomatic older men, vitamin E and/or selenium vs placebo. Dementia incidence did not differ across groups (4.4%). No prevention benefit.

Net: benefit is confined to functional decline in already-diagnosed mild-to-moderate AD. There is no MCI or primary-prevention benefit.

## Safety & interactions

- **All-cause mortality:** Miller 2005 meta-analysis (Ann Intern Med, PMID 15537682), 19 RCTs / ~136,000 participants, found high-dosage vitamin E (>=400 IU/day) associated with increased all-cause mortality and concluded such doses "should be avoided." The AD trial dose (2000 IU/day) is far into this range.
- **Hemorrhagic stroke / bleeding:** Schurks 2010 meta-analysis (BMJ, PMID 21051774), 9 RCTs / 118,765 participants, found hemorrhagic-stroke risk increased (RR ~1.22, +22%) while ischemic-stroke risk fell (RR ~0.90). Net effect is a bleeding-direction hazard.
- **Anticoagulant/antiplatelet interaction:** high-dose vitamin E can potentiate vitamin K antagonists and adds to the bleeding risk of antiplatelets/DOACs; combine only with clinical oversight.
- Avoid high-dose use in people with a hemorrhagic-stroke history or bleeding diathesis. 2000 IU/day is roughly 90x the RDA equivalent.

## Open questions

- Does the TEAM-AD functional benefit replicate outside a predominantly male VA population, and does it persist beyond the trial window?
- Is the AD benefit attributable to a dose that simultaneously carries the mortality/bleeding signal, i.e., is there any therapeutic window?
- Would a lower dose retain any functional benefit while avoiding the >=400 IU/day mortality and hemorrhagic-stroke signals? (unsourced)
- Is dietary/food-source vitamin E (epidemiologically associated with slower decline) meaningfully different from supplemental high-dose alpha-tocopherol? (unsourced for a causal claim)
