## Summary

Iron is an essential trace mineral whose brain-optimization profile is a
**strict deficiency-correction story** — not an enhancement story. Iron
deficiency, with or without anemia, impairs attention and cognition; correcting
that deficiency restores function. The signal is strongest and most replicable
in **iron-deficient women and children** (PMID 20100340; 23744449). In
**iron-replete** people, supplementation provides **no cognitive benefit**, and
iron **overload is actively harmful** — labile iron is redox-active, drives
oxidative stress, and brain iron accumulation is implicated in neurodegeneration
(PMID 26545340).

The overall evidence is **graded 3**: there is good meta-analytic and Cochrane
evidence that repleting deficient/anemic individuals improves cognitive
outcomes, but the benefit is confined to the deficient state, and the harm at
the high end is real. The single most important variable for interpreting any
claim about iron is **iron status**: deficient (benefit from correction) versus
replete (no benefit, potential harm). Iron should be supplemented **only on the
basis of documented deficiency** (low ferritin / iron studies), never as a
generic nootropic.

## Mechanism

- **da (dopaminergic / myelination, graded 3, deficient):** Iron is an obligate
  Fe2+ cofactor for **tyrosine hydroxylase**, the rate-limiting enzyme of
  catecholamine synthesis, so deficiency throttles dopamine (and noradrenaline)
  production. Iron also supports dopamine D1/D2 receptor expression, dopamine
  transporter function, and oligodendrocyte **myelination**. Brain iron
  deficiency in early development produces a dopaminergic-dysfunction phenotype
  and hypomyelination with long-lasting neural and behavioral consequences
  (PMID 12730445; 16770951). This is the leading mechanistic account of the
  attention/cognition deficits seen in iron deficiency — and the basis for why
  *correction* (not supraphysiologic dosing) is what helps.
- **mito (oxygen transport / oxidative metabolism, graded 2, deficient):** Iron
  is central to oxygen delivery (hemoglobin) and to mitochondrial energy
  metabolism via heme and **Fe-S cluster** cofactors of the electron transport
  chain and aconitase. Deficiency reduces cerebral oxidative metabolic capacity,
  the metabolic substrate for cognitive and fatigue symptoms; repletion restores
  it. Critically, the same chemistry cuts the other way in excess — non-transferrin-bound
  iron catalyzes Fenton-reaction reactive oxygen species (PMID 26545340), so the
  direction of benefit is bounded by status.

## Evidence

**Deficiency / anemia cognition — the core benefit.** Falkingham et al. 2010
systematic review and meta-analysis (14 RCTs in older children, adolescents and
women) found oral iron supplementation **improved attention and concentration**
and, in **anemic** participants, improved **IQ by ~2.5 points**; there was **no
effect in non-anemic participants**, nor on memory, psychomotor skills or
scholastic achievement (PMID 20100340). This is the sharpest available statement
of the deficient-vs-replete divide: the cognitive payoff concentrates in the
anemic/deficient subgroup.

**Children with iron-deficiency anemia.** The Wang et al. Cochrane review of iron
therapy in children under three with iron-deficiency anaemia examined
psychomotor development and cognitive function; evidence was limited and did not
demonstrate clear, consistent benefit on developmental indices in this youngest
group, underscoring that timing and reversibility matter and that early-life
deficits may be only partially correctable (PMID 23744449). This is consistent
with the developmental literature showing **long-lasting** effects of early iron
deficiency that repletion may not fully reverse (PMID 16770951).

**Menstruating women.** The Low et al. 2016 Cochrane review (CD009747) of daily
iron supplementation in menstruating women found iron improves haemoglobin and
iron stores and **reduces symptomatic fatigue**, but evidence that it improves
**cognitive performance was uncertain** — trials could not be meta-analysed and
individual results conflicted (PMID 27087396). So even in the population where
deficiency is most prevalent, the cognition-specific signal is weaker than the
fatigue and biochemical signals.

**Replete populations — null.** Across these syntheses, the consistent finding is
that iron supplementation in **non-anemic / iron-replete** people produces **no
cognitive benefit** (PMID 20100340). There is no credible evidence base for iron
as a cognitive enhancer in people with normal iron status, and doing so exposes
them to oxidative and GI harm for no upside.

**Harm in excess (mechanistic / observational).** Iron is redox-active; in
overload, labile iron drives Fenton-reaction reactive oxygen species. Brain iron
**accumulation** is a recognized feature of neurodegenerative disease, and
dysregulated brain iron is implicated in Alzheimer's and Parkinson's pathology
(PMID 26545340). This is why the compound is framed "only if deficient" and why
the overall grade is held at 3 rather than higher.

## Safety & interactions (research metadata)

- **Iron overload is the principal harm.** **Hereditary hemochromatosis** and
  other iron-loading states (transfusional overload, thalassemia) are
  **contraindications** — supplementing adds harmful iron. In excess,
  non-transferrin-bound/labile iron catalyzes reactive oxygen species, and brain
  iron accumulation is mechanistically tied to neurodegeneration (Alzheimer's,
  Parkinson's) (PMID 26545340). Because the body has **no regulated route to
  excrete iron**, overload is easy to cause and hard to undo.
- **Do not supplement non-iron-deficiency anemia.** Anemia of chronic disease or
  B12/folate-deficiency anemia is not corrected by iron; supplementing replete
  stores only adds risk.
- **GI effects (the main adherence barrier).** Oral iron commonly causes
  constipation, nausea, epigastric pain and dark stools; alternate-day dosing can
  improve tolerability and fractional absorption.
- **Acute overdose** is a leading cause of fatal poisoning in young children —
  store securely.
- **Absorption interactions.** Reduced by calcium, antacids/PPIs/H2 blockers
  (gastric acid aids ferric-to-ferrous conversion), tea/coffee polyphenols and
  phytates; **enhanced by vitamin C**. Oral iron chelates and reduces absorption
  of **levothyroxine, tetracycline and quinolone antibiotics, levodopa/methyldopa
  and bisphosphonates** — separate dosing by 2-4 hours. Iron-zinc absorption
  competition at high doses **(unsourced for clinical magnitude)**.

This is research metadata, not medical advice. Supplement only to correct
documented iron deficiency, ideally with monitoring of iron status.

## Open questions

- How fully reversible are the **early-life** cognitive/dopaminergic effects of
  iron deficiency once anemia is corrected, given evidence of long-lasting
  deficits (PMID 16770951; 23744449)?
- Is there any reproducible cognitive benefit from correcting **non-anemic iron
  deficiency** (low ferritin, normal hemoglobin) in adults, distinct from
  fatigue improvement (PMID 27087396)?
- What ferritin/status thresholds best define "deficient enough to benefit"
  versus "replete (no benefit, possible harm)" for cognitive endpoints?
- At a population level, where is the inflection between beneficial repletion and
  harmful brain-iron accumulation, and does long-term low-dose supplementation in
  marginally replete people raise neurodegeneration risk (PMID 26545340)?
