## Summary
"Cognitive challenge & novelty" covers ongoing engagement in mentally effortful, novel activity across life — the behavioral basis of the **cognitive reserve** construct. The strongest, most decision-relevant finding is also a cautionary one: structured cognitive training reliably improves the *trained* tasks, but evidence for **far-transfer** (improvement of untrained, everyday cognition) is weak. The reserve/epidemiological signal (mentally active people decline later) is real but observational and confounded; commercial "brain-training" claims of broad cognitive enhancement are not supported by the consensus literature.

## Mechanism
**ntrophic (primary, up — but modest grade).** The unifying idea is cognitive enrichment building reserve: sustained novel, effortful engagement is thought to build extra network capacity/efficiency that delays the *expression* of age- and pathology-related decline. The animal analogue — environmental enrichment — robustly raises BDNF and hippocampal neurogenesis. In humans, however, the reserve construct is supported by epidemiology (education, occupational complexity, leisure activity predict later decline) rather than by direct neurotrophic measurement, and is heavily confounded by education/SES/reverse-causation. Graded Emerging (2) for this reason.

**glu (contributing, up — and the key to the transfer problem).** Training-induced learning is encoded by NMDA-dependent LTP and synaptic strengthening in the specific engaged circuits. This explains the central, well-replicated finding: training improves the *trained* task because plasticity is task-specific. That same specificity is *why* far-transfer is limited — the strengthened circuitry is the one you practiced, not a general "intelligence" module. Inferred substrate, graded 2.

**da (modulatory — preclinical/inferred).** Novelty and engagement recruit dopaminergic reward/exploration and motivation systems, which gate plasticity and sustain effortful practice; novelty-driven dopamine can enhance hippocampal encoding in imaging studies. Plausible but not isolated as the driver of human reserve outcomes — graded 1.

## Evidence
This entry's value is its honesty about transfer.

- **Trained-task gains: robust. Far-transfer: weak.** Owen et al. 2010 (PMID:20407435), a large online RCT (n≈11,430), found participants improved on the *trained* tasks but those gains did *not* transfer to untrained cognitive tests — even with substantial practice. Simons et al. 2016 (DOI:10.1177/1529100616661983), the major consensus review in Psychological Science in the Public Interest, concluded that brain-training improves performance on trained tasks, shows *less* evidence for near-transfer to related tasks, and *very little* evidence of improvement to everyday cognitive performance — and criticized the commercial industry's marketing claims. This is the load-bearing, decision-relevant finding: do not assume broad cognitive enhancement from brain-training games.
- **Meta-analytic effect is small.** Lampit et al. 2014 (PMID:25405755), pooling 51 studies in healthy older adults, found only a *small* overall effect on untrained cognition (g≈0.22), with the effect modified by training design (e.g. supervised, spaced training did better than unsupervised home use). Small and design-dependent — consistent with limited transfer.
- **ACTIVE — the most durable signal, with caveats.** Rebok et al. 2014 (PMID:24417410), the 10-year follow-up of the ACTIVE RCT (n=2,832 older adults), found that reasoning and speed-of-processing training produced durable improvements *within the trained domain* a decade later, and participants reported less difficulty with instrumental activities of daily living. This is the best evidence that targeted training can yield lasting, partially real-world-relevant benefit — but the gains were largely domain-specific (memory training was weaker), retention was ~44%, and the everyday-function benefit was self-reported. So: durable trained-domain gains (3); broad far-transfer remains unproven.
- **Reserve is observational.** The epidemiology linking lifelong mental engagement to slower decline is consistent but cannot establish causation; education, SES, health behaviors and reverse causation (early subclinical decline reduces engagement) all confound it.

Net: the best-supported, most decision-relevant claims are (a) targeted cognitive training produces durable *trained-domain* gains — ACTIVE, Good (3) — and (b) far-transfer to general/everyday cognition is weak-to-absent (also strongly evidenced). `evidence_overall` is set to **3** on the strength of the durable trained-domain RCT evidence, while the body flags explicitly that broad enhancement / "brain-training works to make you smarter" is **not** supported.

## Safety & interactions (research metadata)
none known. Benign cognitive activity with no contraindications or interactions. The main "risk" is opportunity cost and overstated commercial claims, not physiological harm. This is research metadata, not medical advice.

## Open questions
- Why is far-transfer so limited, and are there any training paradigms (e.g. complex, novel, real-world skills vs. abstract drills) that reliably generalize? Current evidence says mostly no.
- Is cognitive reserve causal, or a marker of education/SES/health and reverse causation? Disentangling requires designs that observational epidemiology cannot provide.
- Does the durable ACTIVE benefit reflect genuine cognitive protection or strategy/skill retention within the trained domain — and does the self-reported IADL benefit hold up to objective measurement?
- What is the human neurotrophic substrate of "reserve"? The BDNF/neurogenesis story is strong in animal enrichment models but largely inferred in humans.
