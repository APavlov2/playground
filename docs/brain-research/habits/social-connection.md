## Summary
Social connection — and its inverse, loneliness/social isolation — is one of the most robust epidemiological signals in this study. Pooled across millions of people, **social isolation and loneliness predict higher all-cause mortality** at a magnitude comparable to established risk factors, and loneliness independently predicts **incident dementia**. The Lancet standing Commission lists social isolation among the 14 modifiable dementia risk factors. The honest asymmetry is that the **observational evidence is strong (4) while the interventional evidence is weak**: trials that successfully *increase* social connection and then show downstream brain/cognitive benefit are scarce, small, and inconsistent, and the cohort associations are vulnerable to reverse causation (early dementia and depression cause withdrawal) and confounding. `evidence_overall: 4` reflects the strength and consistency of the prospective epidemiology for the mortality/dementia-risk association — not a demonstrated causal, modifiable cognitive treatment.

## Mechanism
- **hpa (primary, stress-buffering, down).** The leading account is stress buffering: supportive social contact attenuates HPA-axis and sympathetic responses to acute stressors, while chronic loneliness/isolation is associated with heightened stress reactivity and cumulative allostatic load that is corrosive to other channels. Graded 3 — the buffering mechanism is well-described in humans, but causally linking it to the long-run brain outcomes is the hard part.
- **inflam (down, correlational).** Chronic isolation/loneliness is associated with a conserved transcriptional response to adversity (CTRA): up-regulated pro-inflammatory and down-regulated antiviral gene expression, plus higher inflammatory markers. This is a plausible biological bridge from isolation to cardiometabolic and neurodegenerative risk, but it is correlational in humans — graded emerging (2).
- **ntrophic (up, preclinical/inferred).** Social engagement is hypothesized to build cognitive reserve and maintain synapses; rodent social-isolation models show reduced hippocampal neurogenesis and BDNF. There is no solid human neurotrophic-biomarker evidence, so this is graded preclinical (1).

## Evidence
**Mortality (strongest).** Holt-Lunstad et al., *Perspect Psychol Sci*, 2015 (PMID:25910392) meta-analyzed prospective data on >3.4 million individuals and found social isolation (OR~1.29), loneliness (OR~1.26), and living alone (OR~1.32) each predicted increased mortality, effects that persisted after adjustment and after removing the already-ill. This is consistent, large, and prospective — bar-4 epidemiology.

**Dementia.** Luchetti et al., *Nat Ment Health*, 2024 (PMID:39802418) meta-analyzed >600,000 individuals and found loneliness associated with all-cause dementia (HR~1.31), Alzheimer disease (HR~1.39), and vascular dementia (HR~1.74). The Lancet standing Commission (Livingston et al., *Lancet*, 2024, PMID:39096926) includes **social isolation** among its 14 modifiable dementia risk factors (the full set theoretically accounting for ~49% of cases in aggregate; social isolation is one late-life contributor, not 49% on its own).

**The interventional gap (the key honesty point).** Despite the strong cohort signal, there is **little robust trial evidence** that deliberately increasing social connection improves cognition or lowers dementia incidence. Loneliness-reduction RCTs show modest, mixed effects on loneliness itself and rarely measure hard brain outcomes. The associations are also subject to **reverse causation** — incipient dementia, depression, hearing loss, and frailty all reduce social contact — and to residual confounding. So the relationship is strongly *predictive* but only *plausibly causal/modifiable*.

**Healthy vs impaired.** The risk associations span general adult populations; effects are most studied in older adults for dementia and across the lifespan for mortality. The stress-buffering effect is demonstrable in healthy adults acutely.

Net: best-supported, most relevant claim — social isolation/loneliness prospectively predicts higher mortality and incident dementia — rates **Strong (4)** as epidemiology. The causal, *modifiable-by-intervention* version of the claim is far weaker (~2), and the body says so explicitly.

## Safety & interactions (research metadata)
`none known` — there is no adverse-event profile to increasing healthy social connection. This is research metadata, not medical advice.

## Open questions
- The central gap: do **interventions** that increase social connection actually reduce dementia incidence or slow cognitive decline, or is the cohort signal largely reverse causation/confounding?
- Disentangling **loneliness** (subjective) from **isolation** (objective) — they have partly distinct correlates and may need different interventions.
- Mechanistic weight of **stress-buffering vs inflammation vs cognitive-reserve/engagement** pathways in humans.
- Whether quality vs quantity of relationships, and specific modalities (in-person vs remote), differentially affect brain outcomes.
- Human neurotrophic biomarkers — currently the `ntrophic` link is rodent-only and should not be over-claimed.
