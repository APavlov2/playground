## Summary
Cold-water immersion produces a large, well-documented *acute* noradrenaline/dopamine surge that plausibly explains the alertness and mood-lift people report — but the leap from "acute NE spike" to "better brain health or cognition" is **not supported by controlled human data**. Most of the brain/cognitive narrative is enthusiast-driven, extrapolated from acute physiology, hypothesis papers, or preclinical work. This entry is deliberately graded **down**: the acute catecholamine effect is Emerging (2); durable cognitive/neuroprotective claims are Preclinical/anecdotal (1).

## Mechanism
**ne (up, acute — the one solid effect).** Cold-water immersion strongly activates the sympathetic nervous system: Sramek 2000 measured a ~530% rise in plasma noradrenaline and ~250% in dopamine during 14°C immersion in healthy men. This catecholamine surge is the credible physiological basis for acute arousal, alertness and the post-plunge mood elevation. It is acute and peripheral-plus-central; whether it improves any *cognitive task* is essentially untested in controlled designs.

**hpa (modulate, weak).** Cold is an acute stressor activating the sympatho-adrenal axis; the hormesis hypothesis is that repeated controlled exposure trains stress tolerance and HPA habituation. Mechanistically reasonable, but human evidence specific to brain/stress outcomes is thin and uncontrolled — graded 1.

**inflam (modulate, preclinical).** Claims of anti-inflammatory/oxidative-hormesis benefit rest on preclinical and indirect human signals; nothing brain-relevant is established. Graded 1, population preclinical.

## Evidence
- **Acute catecholamines (Sramek 2000, PMID:10751106).** Controlled immersion study in 10 men across water temperatures; the 14°C condition produced the large NE/DA rises cited above. This is a clean physiological finding — but it is a *biomarker during exposure*, not a cognitive or health outcome. It anchors the `ne` channel at **Emerging (2)** and no higher, because the cognitive consequence is not measured.
- **Mood/depression (Shevchuk 2008, PMID:17993252).** A *hypothesis* paper proposing cold showers for depression, supported by mechanism and anecdote — explicitly not a trial. Cited here only to be transparent that the popular mood claim traces to hypothesis/anecdote, not RCT evidence. It does **not** lift any grade.
- **Cognition / neuroprotection:** there is no body of controlled human trials showing cold exposure improves cognition, memory, or long-term brain health. Small/uncontrolled wellbeing studies and athlete-recovery literature exist but do not test brain outcomes with adequate rigour. Any such claim is marked `unsourced` and graded **Preclinical/anecdotal (1)**.

Net: best-supported, most relevant claim — cold-water immersion causes an acute noradrenergic arousal/mood effect — rates **Emerging (2)**. Durable cognitive or neuroprotective benefit is **unproven** and graded down accordingly; this is the honest position despite the habit's popularity.

## Safety & interactions (research metadata)
Real acute risks: cold-shock response (gasp reflex, hyperventilation, drowning risk) on sudden immersion, transient blood-pressure/cardiac-load spike (cold-pressor effect — caution in cardiovascular disease/uncontrolled hypertension), arrhythmia, and hypothermia with prolonged exposure. This is research metadata, not medical advice.

## Open questions
- Does the acute NE surge translate into any measurable cognitive benefit (vigilance, mood) in a controlled, adequately-blinded design? Currently untested — blinding cold is the core methodological obstacle.
- Is there a durable hormetic adaptation (stress resilience, HPA habituation) from repeated exposure, or only the acute hit? No good human brain-outcome data.
- How much of reported benefit is the catecholamine surge vs expectancy/placebo and the social/ritual context of cold-plunge practice?
- Dose-response (temperature × duration × frequency) for any genuine adaptation is unknown.
