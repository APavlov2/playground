## Summary
Lifelong or long-term language learning (bilingualism) and musical-instrument training are the two best-studied examples of *enriched cognitive activity* and **cognitive reserve**. Both are associated with structural/functional brain differences and, in observational work, with later onset of dementia *symptoms*. The honest picture is split: the structural-plasticity signal (especially in musicians) is robust but largely cross-sectional, while the bilingualism-and-dementia literature is genuinely contested — some meta-analyses find a delay in symptom onset, others find no protection against incidence, and confounding (immigration, education, SES) is hard to rule out.

## Mechanism
**ntrophic (primary, up).** Gaser & Schlaug (2003) found graded grey-matter differences across professional musicians, amateurs and non-musicians in motor, auditory and visuospatial regions, scaling with musicianship status (PMID:14534258) — consistent with experience-dependent structural plasticity from years of audiomotor practice. For bilingualism, structural and functional differences are reported in language-control and executive networks (fronto-parietal, anterior cingulate, basal ganglia). The proposed pathway is the same as for any enriched, demanding activity: repeated, effortful engagement drives use-dependent remodeling and, at the population level, builds *cognitive/neural reserve* — extra functional capacity that postpones the clinical expression of underlying pathology rather than preventing the pathology itself.

**glu (contributing, up — inferred).** The structural phenotype is presumed to rest on NMDA-dependent LTP and dendritic remodeling in repeatedly engaged auditory-motor and language-control circuits. As with skill learning generally, this is inferred from the imaging phenotype, not directly measured in these human cohorts — graded 2.

**ach (modulatory — inferred).** Both activities demand sustained selective attention and fine auditory/phonological encoding, which recruit cholinergic attentional and encoding systems; ACh-gated plasticity is a plausible proximate amplifier. Not isolated in musicians/bilinguals specifically, so graded 1.

## Evidence
Strong on "the brain differs," weaker and contested on "this protects cognition long-term."

- **Musician structural differences (3, but correlational).** Gaser & Schlaug 2003 (PMID:14534258) is a clean cross-sectional VBM study with a dose-like gradient (pro > amateur > non-musician). Cross-sectional design cannot fully exclude self-selection (people with certain brains become musicians), but the gradient and convergence with longitudinal training studies make experience-dependent plasticity the better-supported interpretation. Rated Good (3) for the structural claim; the leap to *general* cognitive enhancement in adults is not established.
- **Bilingualism & dementia — genuinely contested.** Bialystok, Craik & Freedman 2007 (PMID:17125807) reported dementia *symptom onset* roughly 4 years later in bilinguals — but it is a retrospective clinical-records study, vulnerable to referral and immigration confounds. Anderson et al. 2020 meta-analysis (DOI:10.3758/s13423-020-01736-5) found a moderate effect on age of AD symptom onset (d≈0.32) but only a weak effect on disease incidence (d≈0.10). Crucially, Mukadam et al. 2017 (PMID:28387680) found that *prospective* studies show **no** significant protective effect on dementia incidence (OR≈0.96, CI crossing 1). The reconciliation most authors reach: bilingualism may *delay the clinical expression* of dementia (a reserve effect on symptom onset) without lowering the underlying disease risk — and even that is not unanimous. Graded honestly: the symptom-delay/reserve claim is Good-ish (3) but with explicit dissent; the "prevents dementia" claim is **not supported**.
- **Confounding is the core problem.** Education, socioeconomic status, immigration history and overall cognitive engagement co-travel with both bilingualism and musicianship, and reserve is hard to separate from those. Channels are mapped indirectly, so glu/ach are graded down.

Net: best-supported, most relevant claim — sustained instrument training and lifelong bilingualism are associated with experience-dependent structural plasticity and, for bilingualism, a likely *delay in dementia symptom onset* (reserve) — rates **Good (3)**. The stronger claim that they *prevent* dementia or broadly raise cognition is not supported and is flagged.

## Safety & interactions (research metadata)
none known. These are benign cognitive activities with no contraindications or interactions. This is research metadata, not medical advice.

## Open questions
- Does bilingualism delay dementia symptom onset causally, or is the association an artifact of education/immigration/SES confounding? Prospective and incidence data are discordant.
- Reserve vs. enhancement: do these activities add functional buffer against future pathology, improve current cognition, or both? The evidence favors reserve/symptom-delay over broad current enhancement.
- For musicians, how much of the structural difference is experience-driven vs. self-selection — disentangling requires more longitudinal training designs.
- Dose-response: how much practice, started at what age, is needed to accrue a reserve benefit, and does adult-onset language/instrument learning confer the same effect as lifelong practice?
