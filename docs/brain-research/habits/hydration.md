## Summary
Hydration is a deficit-correction habit: being well-hydrated does not boost an already-euhydrated brain above baseline — but *mild* dehydration (≈1–2% body-mass loss, reachable through an ordinary day's inadequate intake) measurably degrades attention, vigilance, working memory and mood, and correcting it restores performance. The human evidence for the decrement is good (controlled studies in both sexes plus a meta-analysis). The framing matters: the win is removing a deficit, not adding an enhancement.

## Mechanism
**cbf (up — deficit correction).** Hypohydration lowers plasma volume and can reduce cerebral blood flow while raising the perceived effort of tasks. This is one plausible substrate for the cognitive cost; rehydration normalises it. The direction is "up" only in the sense of restoring perfusion from a depleted state.

**ne / attention (modulate — deficit correction).** The most reliable behavioural signature of mild dehydration is degraded sustained attention/vigilance with increased fatigue and effort. The mechanism is not a clean noradrenergic action — it likely blends perfusion, osmotic signalling, and arousal/motivational effort — so this channel is graded "modulate," reflecting restoration of attentional function rather than upregulation of arousal per se.

## Evidence
- **Women (Armstrong 2012, PMID:22190027).** 25 healthy young women, placebo-controlled crossover across euhydrated vs mildly dehydrated states (~1.4% body-mass loss): dehydration worsened mood, increased fatigue/headache, and degraded aspects of cognition. Small but well-controlled.
- **Men (Ganio 2011, PMID:21736786).** Companion design in 26 healthy young men (~1.6% loss): impaired vigilance and working memory, increased difficulty/anxiety on tasks. Together the two give a within-subject, both-sex signal that *mild* deficit harms attention.
- **Meta-analysis (Wittbrodt & Millard-Stafford 2018, PMID:29528918).** Pooled studies found dehydration of ~>2% body mass significantly impairs cognitive performance, with attention, executive function and motor coordination most affected. Multiple human studies, consistent direction → supports **Good (3)** for the *decrement-and-its-correction* claim.

Net: best-supported, most relevant claim — *mild dehydration impairs attention/vigilance/working memory and rehydration corrects it* — rates **Good (3)**. There is no credible evidence that hyper-hydrating beyond normal improves cognition; the effect is purely deficit-correction (and over-drinking carries its own risk).

## Safety & interactions (research metadata)
The main risk is over-correction: excessive water intake without electrolytes can cause hyponatremia, itself dangerous to the brain. No drug interactions of note for ordinary hydration. This is research metadata, not medical advice.

## Open questions
- How small a deficit matters in everyday (non-exercise) conditions, and how much of the lab effect is driven by the discomfort/awareness of thirst rather than physiology (blinding hydration state is hard)?
- Do chronically lower-end-of-normal hydrators show persistent cognitive cost, or only acute, fully-reversible decrements?
- Individual variation by age (older adults have blunted thirst) and by task type.
- Cleanly separating perfusion vs osmotic vs effort/motivation mechanisms.
