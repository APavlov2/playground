## Summary
Resistance (strength) training is treated in this study as the established #1 lever for keeping the brain growing and repairing, working primarily through the neurotrophic/neurogenesis channel (IGF-1 and BDNF signaling that support hippocampal neurogenesis and synaptic remodeling). In humans the most reproducible cognitive signal is improved **executive function** — selective attention and conflict resolution — shown in randomized controlled trials in older adults and supported by meta-analysis of exercise interventions in adults over 50. Benefits accrue over months of consistent progressive training rather than acutely. Effects are clearest in aging/at-risk populations; in already-high-functioning young adults the cognitive gains are smaller and less consistently measured.

## Mechanism
**ntrophic (primary, up).** Mechanical loading of muscle stimulates secretion of insulin-like growth factor-1 (IGF-1), which crosses into the CNS and, together with exercise-induced BDNF, drives the neurotrophic cascade — hippocampal neurogenesis in the dentate gyrus, synaptogenesis, dendritic arborization and angiogenesis. This BDNF/IGF-1 neurogenesis axis is why strength training is taken as the headline lever. The honest caveat: human BDNF elevation is best documented for exercise broadly (Szuhany 2015, PMID:25455510, reports a small but reliable rise in resting BDNF, Hedges' g≈0.27, larger acutely), and direct measurement of human adult neurogenesis is not feasible in trials — so the neurogenesis story rests on preclinical IGF-1/BDNF work plus human cognition and brain-structure RCTs rather than on a direct human neurogenesis readout. Graded **3** on that combined human-plus-mechanistic basis, not 4.

**mito (up).** Resistance training raises mitochondrial content and metabolic efficiency in muscle and improves systemic insulin sensitivity and glucose handling, which supports stable cerebral substrate delivery. The brain-specific bioenergetic benefit is inferred from systemic metabolic improvement rather than measured directly, hence **2**.

**inflam (down).** Chronic resistance training lowers systemic low-grade inflammatory markers (CRP, IL-6) over time, and contracting muscle releases a net anti-inflammatory myokine profile; this plausibly lowers microglial inflammatory tone. CNS-specific evidence is indirect, so **2**.

**hpa (modulate).** Over weeks, regular training improves stress-axis regulation — better cortisol recovery and lower resting sympathetic tone — even though a single heavy session acutely raises cortisol. Net chronic effect is modulatory and graded **2**.

## Evidence
- **Northey JM et al., 2018 (PMID:28438770)** — systematic review with meta-analysis of exercise interventions for cognition in adults older than 50. Resistance-training interventions improved overall cognitive measures; the review concluded both aerobic and resistance exercise benefit cognition, with effects most evident versus passive (wait-list) controls and attenuated versus active controls. This is the strongest aggregate human support and anchors the **3** grade.
- **Liu-Ambrose T et al., 2010 (PMID:20101012)** — single-blind 12-month RCT in 155 community-dwelling women aged 65-75. Once- or twice-weekly progressive resistance training improved performance on the Stroop test (selective attention/conflict resolution, an executive function) relative to a balance-and-tone control. A direct, well-controlled human RCT showing a domain-specific executive benefit from resistance training specifically.
- **Szuhany KL et al., 2015 (PMID:25455510)** — meta-analysis (29 studies, N≈1111) confirming exercise raises peripheral BDNF: moderate acute rise after a single session (Hedges' g≈0.46), and a small increase in resting BDNF with regular training (g≈0.27). Establishes the human BDNF mechanism but for exercise broadly, not resistance-only — a reason the ntrophic grade is held at 3 rather than 4.

Net: best-supported, most relevant claim — resistance training improves executive function in older adults — rates **Good (3)** (multiple RCTs plus a meta-analysis). The neurogenesis mechanism is mechanistically strong but not directly measured in humans, so the badge stays at 3 rather than 4. In young high-functioning adults the cognitive payoff is smaller and the literature thinner.

## Safety & interactions (research metadata)
Resistance training is broadly safe and recommended across age groups. Main injury/overtraining metadata: musculoskeletal strain or joint injury from poor technique or aggressive load progression; transient large blood-pressure spikes during maximal efforts or Valsalva straining (relevant in uncontrolled hypertension or unstable cardiovascular disease, where clearance is advised); and blunted adaptation with chronic under-recovery/overtraining. No drug interactions in the pharmacological sense. This is research metadata, not medical advice.

## Open questions
- How much of the cognitive benefit is resistance-specific versus shared with aerobic exercise? Most large meta-analyses pool or compare modalities and cannot fully isolate strength training.
- Does the IGF-1/BDNF neurogenesis mechanism operate at the doses people actually train at, and does it translate into durable hippocampal-structure change in humans (as aerobic training does in Erickson 2011)? Direct human neurogenesis data remain unavailable.
- Optimal dose: a recent network meta-analysis suggested twice-weekly ~45-min sessions over ~12 weeks for global cognition, but dose-response is not settled.
- Magnitude and reliability of cognitive benefit in young, already-high-functioning adults is under-studied relative to the older-adult literature.
- Independent contribution of resistance versus combined (concurrent aerobic + resistance) training to brain outcomes.
