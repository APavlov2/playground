## Summary
Treating age-related hearing loss with hearing aids is a deficit-correction habit: it does not enhance an intact brain, it removes a well-established risk driver from an impaired one. The 2024 Lancet Commission ranks hearing loss as the single largest *attributable* modifiable midlife dementia risk factor (~7% of the ~45% theoretically preventable fraction). The ACHIEVE RCT (Lin 2023) is the key causal test: in the full cohort the intervention did **not** slow 3-year cognitive decline, but in the prespecified higher-risk subgroup it slowed decline by ~48%. The benefit is therefore real but conditional — strongest in those already at elevated risk.

## Mechanism
**ntrophic (primary, up — by deprivation reversal).** The leading mechanistic accounts for the hearing-loss/dementia link are (1) *sensory-deprivation*: chronically degraded auditory input is associated with accelerated atrophy of auditory and downstream cortex; (2) *cognitive-load*: effortful listening diverts working-memory/executive resources from encoding; and (3) *cascade*: hearing loss drives social isolation and depression, themselves risk factors. Hearing aids act on (1) and (2) by restoring afferent input. This is reserve *preservation* (removing a disuse driver), not a positive growth signal — hence the channel direction is "up" only in the sense of correcting a deficit. Which mechanism dominates is unresolved.

**inflam (down, weak/speculative).** Any anti-inflammatory benefit would be indirect, via reduced isolation/depression-related stress load. Not demonstrated for the intervention itself — graded 1 (mechanistic only).

## Evidence
- **Causal RCT — ACHIEVE (Lin 2023, PMID:37478886).** 977 adults aged 70–84 with untreated hearing loss, randomised to a best-practice hearing intervention vs a health-education control, 3-year follow-up. **Primary result: no significant difference in 3-year global cognitive decline in the total cohort.** A prespecified analysis by recruitment source showed the cohort comprised an older, higher-cardiovascular-risk group (from the ARIC study, n≈238) and healthier de-novo community volunteers (n≈739). In the higher-risk ARIC group the intervention slowed decline by ~48%; in the healthy volunteers there was no effect. Interpretation per the authors: hearing intervention may protect cognition specifically in those at elevated risk. This split-result is why the claim is graded **Good (3)**, not Strong (4) — the headline primary endpoint was null.
- **Epidemiology / attributable risk (Livingston 2024, PMID:39096926).** The Lancet standing Commission models hearing loss as the largest single attributable midlife modifiable factor. This is observational/modelled population-attributable-fraction work, not proof of causation — its weight comes from consistency across cohorts, not from intervention data.
- **Cohort association (Lin 2011, PMID:21320988).** In the Baltimore Longitudinal Study of Aging, baseline hearing loss predicted incident dementia in a dose-dependent manner — foundational association, observational, confounding-prone.

Net: the best-supported, decision-relevant claim — *treating hearing loss slows cognitive decline in higher-risk older adults* — rates **Good (3)** on the strength of one large RCT with a positive prespecified subgroup but a null primary endpoint. In low-risk healthy older adults the cognitive benefit is currently unproven (≈2).

## Safety & interactions (research metadata)
No medical contraindications to hearing rehabilitation as such. Practical limits: benefit is conditional on actually wearing the device (adherence), cost/access barriers, and fitting comfort. No drug interactions. This is research metadata, not medical advice.

## Open questions
- Does the ACHIEVE subgroup effect replicate in a trial powered specifically on a high-risk population? The current positive signal is a prespecified secondary analysis.
- Is the benefit prevention of decline, or improvement of test performance via better task audibility? Trials use design features to mitigate this but it is not fully resolved.
- Optimal timing — does earlier intervention (mid-life) yield larger protection than the 70–84 window studied?
- Do cochlear implants (for severe loss) confer similar or larger cognitive protection? Largely uncontrolled data so far.
